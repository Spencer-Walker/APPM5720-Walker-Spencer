Goal: Solve Maxwell's equations for a gaussian beam hitting a gas jet.

For the simplest version of this problem the nuclei are fixed and all charge is "bound". So rho and J in Maxwell's macroscopic equations are zero. The polarization density is just the density of atoms ( or molecules) times their total dipole moment at a point. 

As a simple model the gas jet can be treated as an infinite slab of charge with some distribution in the propagation direction of the laser pulse (\hat{z}), and the beam can be treated as a function of z and t, and is polarized in the \hat{x} direction. Since mu = 1 this is a 1D wave equation for the electric field sourced by the second derivitive of the polarization density ( the molecular density times the dipole acceleration). 

Various different models can be used for the dipole accleration. The simplest is to assume that the gas is just a non-interacing free electron gas. In this model the dipole accleration is poportional to the electric field. In more realistic (perturbative) models odd order susceptibilities are included as non-linear sources to the wave equation. In the real problem finite order susceptibilities are not sufficient since the field strengths are large enough not to be purtubitive. In the strong field case the time-dependent schrodinger equation needs to be solved at every gridpoint for the maxwell solver in order to accurately describe the polarization density.

Right now I'm going to use a toy model or 1D Hydrogen atoms to calculate the dipole acceleration at every point in the gas and will play with various interpolation schemes to reduce the number of TDSE solves needed for the gas. I will try simple things such as just solving fewer atoms and using b-splines to interpolate in space (which I haven't seen done) and Computing a Jacobian and Hessian with respect to the macroscopic position  (indexes into an atom in the gas) of the polarization density and propagating the macroscopic gradient of the wavefunction (each atom locally sees a constant field (dipole approximation) but the field changes across the entire gas) and propagating that (has been done before). In the later scheme the gas is broken up into elements where the TDSE is solved in the middle and the polarization throughout the element is approximated using more or less taylor series. This is a local scheme I might try other global schemes? 

Once the toy problem is done I'm going to make various maxwell solvers and schrodinger solvers that can be interchanged for different problems. For example a 3D gas and eventually a solid (mu may not be 1) on the maxwell side and a classical atoms, 1D quantum atoms, 3D atoms, and density functional theory for molecules in order to model the polarization density. 

For the scrodinger solver I don't anticipate needing more than one node per atom so I'm most likely going to either use operator some split-step operator method or fourth order Lobatto with variable timesteps for integrating in time. This makes the time evolution operator not unitary (probability is not conserved) but for most problems it is negligable.

For the space discritization I will start with the simplest thing which is 4th order finite difference, but instead of changing the first row of the second derivative operator to a forward difference (which makes the hamiltonian not hermitian) I'm going to take advantage of the analytic form of the radial solutions at very small r in order to generate a hermitian stencil near the origin. A center difference should be fine near the end of the box since the wavefunction is assumed to be zero (after the absorbing region). 

I might also use a grid that grows over time if the electrons far away from the origin are needed for the polarization density. Some groups use a small box where the electrons that are absorbed by exterior scaling are treated as a classical distribution of charge and included in Maxwell's macroscopic equations by the inclusion of free charge and free charge densities.
